## One-sentence explanation

Singapore's economy is the connected activity through which people and firms produce goods and services here, sell to local and overseas customers, and generate output, jobs and incomes. [[S1]](#s1)

## 30-second overview

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** measures the value of goods and services produced within Singapore's economic territory. It is a useful measure of production, but not a complete score for every household's wellbeing or every effect of economic activity. [[S1]](#s1)

Both services and goods-producing industries are part of the picture. SingStat publishes industry-level data, including wholesale trade, manufacturing, finance and insurance, transport and storage, and information and communications. [[S1]](#s1) [[S2]](#s2)

## The big picture

    People, firms and capabilities
                 ↓
     Goods-producing + services industries
                 ↓
       Local demand + trade with the world
                 ↓
          Output, jobs and incomes

This is a map for asking better questions, not a promise that every industry or household experiences the economy in the same way. GDP is one measure within the map. [[S1]](#s1)

## The five-minute explanation

### GDP: a measure with a specific job

SingStat defines GDP as the aggregate value of goods and services produced within Singapore's economic territory. It compiles GDP using output, expenditure and income approaches. [[S1]](#s1) In plain English, GDP helps describe how much production took place here during a period.

That definition also marks its boundary. GDP alone does not tell a reader how income is shared between households, how much unpaid work occurred, or the full environmental impact of activity. This is a scope explanation based on what GDP measures; it is not an argument that GDP is unimportant. [[S1]](#s1)

### Why sectors matter

Looking at industries gives more texture than a single GDP number. SingStat's economy material groups activity into services and goods-producing industries, and provides sector detail. [[S1]](#s1) [[S2]](#s2) A sector share or growth rate is always a dated statistic: readers should check the release period and definition before using it in a conversation.

### Productivity is not simply “working harder”

SingStat presents value added per worker as a productivity measure. [[S1]](#s1) For a first reading, productivity asks how much output is produced relative to an input such as labour. It should not be reduced to a judgment about whether individual workers tried hard enough; the precise measure and period matter.

### A practical example: a business and the wider economy

Imagine a firm that designs a service in Singapore, employs people here, buys some inputs from overseas, and sells to local and overseas customers.

1. Its work is part of production within Singapore's economic territory.
2. Its activity sits in an industry grouping used in economic statistics.
3. Its local and overseas transactions show why domestic activity and trade can be connected.
4. Its output contributes to measures such as GDP, but GDP does not by itself describe every worker's or customer's experience.

This is an orientation example, not a statement about a particular company, sector, income or economic forecast.

## Why people care

Economic terms appear in conversations about jobs, wages, prices, business opportunities and public resources. A useful first question is: **what exactly does this number measure, over what period, and for whom?** That stops a GDP headline from carrying more meaning than it can support.

## Singapore / Southeast Asia angle

Singapore's economic activity is connected to both local demand and trade with the world. This baseline explains that connection without ranking Singapore, predicting performance, or comparing countries.

## Discussion points, not positions

- Which measure is being cited: output, prices, employment, productivity or something else?
- What period and definition does it use?
- Does a headline describe the whole economy, one sector, or one group's experience?
- What benefits and trade-offs are missing from a single measure?

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **“GDP is the same as wellbeing.”** GDP measures production; it is not a complete account of wellbeing or distribution. [[S1]](#s1)
- **“One sector's figure describes every business.”** Industry statistics are grouped, dated measures. [[S1]](#s1) [[S2]](#s2)
- **“Productivity means workers should simply work harder.”** It is a measure of output relative to inputs, with a stated method and period. [[S1]](#s1)
- **“A current forecast belongs in an evergreen explainer.”** Forecasts and changing figures need a date and a current, attributable source.
