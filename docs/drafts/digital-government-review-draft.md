## One-sentence explanation

Digital Government is Singapore's effort to make public transactions easier, more seamless, secure and accessible, using shared digital services such as Singpass while recognising that people also need access and support. [[S1]](#s1) [[S2]](#s2) [[S4]](#s4)

## 30-second overview

When a person or business needs a public service, four ideas are worth separating: the **service** is the task; **digital identity** can establish who is using it; **authorisation** can establish what someone may do for an organisation; and **digital inclusion** covers the access, connectivity and skills that make participation possible. [[S1]](#s1) [[S2]](#s2) [[S3]](#s3) [[S4]](#s4)

Singpass is a trusted digital identity used to access government and business services online. It is important, but it is not the same thing as every government service, nor does it decide whether someone is eligible for that service. [[S2]](#s2)

## The five-minute explanation

### A service is the task; Singpass is one part of how a person may enter it

It is easy to use “Singpass” as shorthand for digital government. That hides an important distinction. A digital service is the public task itself: for example, finding information, making a submission or checking the progress of an application. Singpass is a trusted digital identity that can help a person access participating government and business services online. [[S1]](#s1) [[S2]](#s2)

A question about whether a service is understandable is about service design. A question about safely proving identity is about trust. A question about whether a person can use the service at all may be about access or skills. The questions can overlap, but they are not identical.

### Identity is not the same as authorisation

**Identity** answers a question like “who is using this service?” **Authorisation** answers a different question: “what may this person do for an organisation?” GovTech's description of the National Digital Identity places Singpass alongside Corppass, the corporate authorisation system. [[S3]](#s3)

This matters when a discussion involves a business or organisation. A person may be able to prove who they are without automatically being allowed to act for every organisation. The exact rules for a particular transaction are service-specific and should be checked on its official page.

### Designing for less repetition does not remove the need for care

GovTech identifies authentication, pre-filled information for participating services, and digital signing as parts of the Singpass experience. [[S2]](#s2) At a high level, these shared capabilities can reduce repeated steps across services. They do not mean every service collects the same information, works in the same way, or has the same eligibility conditions.

### Digital inclusion belongs inside the model

Digital delivery works best only when people can reach and use it. IMDA's Digital Access work covers access to technology and digital skills, not just the existence of an online portal. [[S4]](#s4) This makes inclusion a core design concern rather than an afterthought.

### A practical example: starting a public-service task

Imagine someone needs to complete a public-service task.

1. They identify the official service and what it is asking them to do: the **service** layer. [[S1]](#s1)
2. If the service requires it, they use a trusted identity to enter the participating service: the **identity** layer. [[S2]](#s2)
3. If the task is for an organisation, the relevant authorisation rules may matter as well: the **authorisation** layer. [[S3]](#s3)
4. If access to a device, connection or digital skills is a barrier, the **inclusion** layer is part of the real-world outcome. [[S4]](#s4)

This is an orientation example, not a promise about a specific service, eligibility outcome, data field, security feature or assisted channel.

## Why people care

Digital public services affect ordinary interactions with government and businesses. A good discussion can consider convenience and reduced repetition, but also clarity, trust, privacy, accessibility and whether people can participate without being left behind.

## Singapore / Southeast Asia angle

This Briefing explains Singapore's own public-service and digital-identity context. It does not rank Singapore against other countries or make claims about regional digital-government systems. Those comparisons need comparable, dated evidence and their own scope.

## Discussion points, not positions

- Is the issue about the service itself, identity, authorisation, data use, or access and skills?
- What would make the service clearer for a first-time user without weakening safeguards?
- Who may find a fully digital route difficult to use, and what current official support is available?
- Which part of the discussion is a durable design question, and which part is a changing operational detail?

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **“Singpass is every government service.”** It is a trusted digital identity used to access participating services, not the service itself. [[S2]](#s2)
- **“Identity and authorisation mean the same thing.”** They answer different questions, especially for organisational transactions. [[S3]](#s3)
- **“An online service is automatically accessible to everyone.”** Access to devices, connectivity and digital skills also matters. [[S4]](#s4)
- **“A general explainer can provide current account or security steps.”** Use current official guidance for these operational details.
